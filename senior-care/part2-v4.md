# Part 2: How Real Estate Companies Are Playing Senior Living

## 2.1 The moves: who is building what

Over the last two years, almost every major developer in India has announced something in senior living. Let's go through the money being put to work.

**DLF**, India's largest listed real estate company, is planning its first senior living project in Sector 63, Gurugram. It covers about 5 lakh square feet, and the company expects around ₹2,000 crore in sales from it. That works out to roughly ₹40,000 per square foot, and Colliers says homes here are expected to be priced above ₹12 crore each. As of August 2026, the launch was held up by regulatory approvals.

**Max Estates** launched 292 senior homes at Estate 360 in Gurugram, and all of them sold out in 11 months. Gross sales came to about ₹1,530 crore, at roughly ₹20,000 per square foot, or about ₹5 crore per home. Its next project, Estate 361, launched in December 2025 at around ₹22,000 per square foot. By March 2026, 127 of the 180 phase-one homes were sold.

**Ashiana Housing** has made senior living its core business. It sold ₹570 crore of senior homes in FY26, up about 49% from ₹383 crore in FY25. Five years ago senior living was about 10% of Ashiana's business. Today it is 23%, and management says the mix will shift "decisively" towards senior living over the next two to three years. The company has also bought its largest-ever land parcel in Pune for a senior project. But Ashiana plays a very different market: its 569 senior homes in FY26 averaged about ₹1 crore each, or roughly ₹7,700 per square foot.

**Brigade Group** has partnered with Primus Senior Living on three projects with over 600 homes and a gross development value of about ₹750 crore.

**Columbia Pacific's Serene Communities** is investing about ₹3,000 crore across six cities. It has around 2,600 homes under construction across 13 projects, priced between ₹70 lakh and ₹1.5 crore.

And there are newer entrants. **Hiranandani Group** has announced 'Elements', a 400-residence senior project near Chennai. **Godrej Properties** has partnered with Manasum Senior Living for 'Banyan' in North Bengaluru. **Paranjape Schemes** already runs 16 senior communities under its Athashri brand.

Now put the prices side by side. Ashiana at about ₹7,700 per square foot. Max Estates at ₹20,000 to ₹22,000. DLF at about ₹40,000. That is a fivefold gap within the same product category. So senior living is really two markets: a mid-market in Tier 2 and peripheral cities, and a luxury market inside the metros, close to large hospitals and to the children.

### What kind of capex this is

Notice what all these moves have in common. They are classic real estate capex: buy land, get approvals, build, sell. The money goes into land and construction, and it comes back once, when the home is sold.

The model is also very efficient on cash. According to Max India's investor presentation, more than 95% of homes in such projects are sold during construction. The buyer's advance funds the building. For a developer, senior living is simply a new premium product line on the same balance sheet.

So the obvious question is: if homes are selling this well, what is the problem?

## 2.2 The problem with build-and-sell

The problem is that in senior living, the building is not the product. The service is.

A family buying a senior home is not paying ₹5 crore for three bedrooms. It is paying for what happens inside them: a nurse who responds at 2 am, a doctor on call, meals suited to a diabetic, an ambulance that reaches a tertiary hospital within the "golden hour", and people to talk to. Colliers puts it plainly. Senior living is "not a build-and-sell product rather it is an operating business".

That creates four problems for a developer who builds, sells and leaves.

**First, the revenue ends exactly when the need begins.** The developer earns at handover. The resident's care needs then rise for the next 15 to 20 years. All the value created in those years goes to whoever runs the community, not whoever built it.

**Second, trust follows the operator, not the builder.** Families choose senior living on reputation. Colliers estimates that a trusted operator gets 30 to 50% of new residents through referrals from existing residents, doctors and nurses. A developer that has moved on to its next project cannot build that kind of reputation.

**Third, outright sale shrinks the market.** Buying a ₹1–5 crore home is out of reach for most seniors, and in India renting in old age still carries a stigma. That is a big reason penetration is only about 1.5% of the 17 lakh affluent urban senior households. Selling homes only reaches the top of the pyramid.

**Fourth, the asset depends on the service.** A senior home can only be resold to another senior, and only if the community is well run. If the services decline, occupancy falls, resale values fall and the brand suffers. The developer's product keeps its value only if someone else does the hard work for decades.

In short, build-and-sell captures the easiest money in this sector, once, and leaves the long-term value on the table.

That is why the second kind of capex is now growing: companies are putting money into running senior living, not just building it.

## 2.3 The shift to operations

### What the operations model looks like

In the operations model, the developer builds and sells the homes, and a specialist operator runs the community and the care: maintenance, food, activities, security, emergency response and nursing. The operator does not need to own the land. It earns fees and recurring income instead.

Max Group shows this most clearly, because it has split the model into two listed companies. Max Estates builds and sells the homes. Max India's subsidiary, Antara Senior Living, runs them.

Look at how Antara earns, according to Max India's June 2026 investor presentation:
- an 8–10% project management fee on each project,
- a 20–25% EBITDA margin on maintenance income once a community stabilises, and
- resale fees. At its Dehradun community, five homes changed hands in FY26 and Antara earned about ₹1.71 crore in fees.

Antara put no money into the land, yet it earns three ways, and keeps earning for as long as the community exists.

Operators also make new payment models possible, which is how the market can grow beyond outright buyers. Colliers describes three:
- **Deferred ownership:** the senior pays a refundable deposit of 50–70% of the home's value for lifetime occupancy, and the deposit goes back to the heirs.
- **Subscription:** a lower base price, with meals, housekeeping or 24-hour nursing added as paid tiers.
- **Buyback:** the operator commits to buy back or resell the home at pre-agreed terms when the resident leaves.

All three only work if someone is running the community for the long term.

### What other countries did

In mature markets, this shift happened long ago.

- **United States:** "active adult" rental communities became their own asset class, and communities moved from suburban campuses into vertical buildings in city centres.
- **United Kingdom:** pension funds became owners. In late 2025, NatWest Pension Trustees bought 100% of Inspired Villages from Legal & General, to hold an operating senior living business for the long term.
- **China:** insurers became developers. With Taikang Insurance, you buy a whole-life policy. At retirement there is no cash payout. Instead, you or your parents get guaranteed admission to a premium Taikang retirement community.
- **Japan:** the state built it into the system through long-term care insurance, and care networks designed to be within 30 minutes of a senior's home.

The pattern is the same everywhere. A patient, long-term investor such as a pension fund, insurer or REIT owns the buildings, and a specialist operator runs them. It looks a lot like hotels, where one party owns the property and another runs the brand.

India hasn't seen a landmark private equity acquisition of a senior living operator yet. Colliers says the sector is still in its developer-led phase, and expects developer-operator partnerships to become mainstream within five years.

### Who is moving in India

The shift has already started.

- **Primus Senior Living** works with Brigade, Wadhwa, Dosti Realty, Srijan and Naiknavare, and has 9 projects across six cities. It has set up a ₹2,000 crore senior living platform with HDFC Capital, one of the clearest signs of institutional money entering the sector.
- **Serene Communities** manages about 1,700 homes across 10 communities and partners with Embassy, TVS Emerald and Asset Homes. It has also opened a 104-bed assisted living facility in Bengaluru's Electronic City, where a one-bedroom unit with medical care is rented at ₹50,000–75,000 a month, with the medical side handled by KITES Senior Care.
- **Godrej Properties** chose to partner with an operator, Manasum, rather than run the community itself.
- **Prestige Group**, according to Colliers, is planning to enter hospitality-led senior living on a rental model and has hired a leadership team to build the platform. That is a large developer choosing to rent, not sell.
- Even **Ashiana**, the strongest build-and-operate player, partners with Epoch Elder Care for assisted living and dementia care.

### Why operations is harder

None of this is easy, and the numbers show it.

An operating platform earns slowly. Colliers says a senior living project takes four to six years to go from feasibility to stable occupancy, while Indian real estate capital usually wants quick returns.

Care is also the hardest layer to make profitable. Antara runs 485 care home beds across four cities. Revenue grew about 1.6 times in Q4 FY26, but average occupancy was only 27%. Some beds were added recently, which pulls the number down, but as a result Max India was still EBITDA-negative at the consolidated level in FY26.

Part of the reason is cultural. Max India's presentation sums it up as "kids pay, parents use". Children pay, parents have to move, and persuading parents to leave their own home is still hard. Operators also face real risks: caregiver attrition, missing the golden hour in an emergency, and hidden charges that destroy trust.

Still, the direction is clear. JLL expects rental and service-led models to overtake outright sale within three to five years. Remember the gap from Part 1: about 2,100 assisted living beds today against roughly 11,000 needed by 2030. The biggest shortage sits exactly where the operating model is hardest, and the companies that can deliver care profitably will be the ones that win.
