# History of Kerala's Gulf Migration and How It Transformed the State

Scope note: the core numbers come from the Kerala Migration Surveys (KMS: 1998, 2003, 2008, 2013, 2018, 2023), run first by CDS (K.C. Zachariah and S. Irudaya Rajan) and in 2023 by IIMAD (Irudaya Rajan). KMS numbers are sample-survey estimates and are revised between rounds (for example, 1998 is given as 1.32m in one paper and 1.4m in the 2023 summary). Say "about" when using them on screen.

## 1. Before the Gulf: Arab trade, Mappilas, and migration to Ceylon, Malaya, Burma and Bombay

### Takeaway
Kerala's link to Arabia is about 2,000 years old. Malabar ports traded with the Gulf from the 1st century AD, and Omani sailors married into Calicut families. Malayalis were migrating for work (to Ceylon, Burma, Aden, Penang, Basra, Singapore/Malaya and Bombay) long before the oil boom. Malabar Muslim seafarers and dock workers were already in Gulf ports by the 1920s–60s.

### Cited Findings
- Archaeological evidence from Pattanam shows Kerala–Gulf trade from the 1st century AD. In the 14th century Ibn Battuta recorded Arab settlements in Kerala's coastal towns. Medieval sources describe Omani sailors who "married women from major port towns in Malabar, mainly Calicut." — [Ilias, OMER Journal 2018](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- Most local families in Khor Fakkan (UAE) had "experience of voyages to Calicut via dhows". This was one reason Khor Fakkan became the main landing point for Malayali migrants. — [Ilias, OMER Journal 2018](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- Arab traders kept ties with Kozhikode from the 14th to the 18th centuries. — [The National, "The Gulf Wives of Kerala"](https://www.thenationalnews.com/arts/the-gulf-wives-of-kerala-1.550611)
- In the late 1800s, Keralite labourers were working in Ceylon, Burma, Aden and Southeast Asia. British Library pamphlets titled "Basrapattu, Pinangupattu" (songs of Basra and Penang) record Basra and Penang as destinations. — [Scroll, "The 'Malayali on the moon' and fault lines of caste"](https://scroll.in/article/1068343/the-malayali-on-the-moon-and-fault-lines-of-caste-what-drove-generations-of-migration-from-kerala)
- For lower-caste (avarna) migrants, working abroad brought a kind of freedom. The work was hard, but they "did not face the contempt that they did back home." Migration was an escape from "the rigid grip of hierarchy and humiliation." — [Scroll](https://scroll.in/article/1068343/the-malayali-on-the-moon-and-fault-lines-of-caste-what-drove-generations-of-migration-from-kerala)
- Earlier migrants to Singapore, Malaysia and Sri Lanka were known as "Singapore Yatrakar." — [The News Minute](https://www.thenewsminute.com/kerala/how-boat-journey-mid-1960s-started-kerala-s-gulf-boom-130847)
- **Anecdote (a pre-oil-boom Gulf migrant):** Abdulkader left South Malabar in the late 1920s and worked in Lahore, Muscat and Qatar before settling in Ajman. His brother Aboobacker came looking for him and reached Ajman in 1963. — [Ilias, OMER Journal 2018](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- **Anecdote (racism in the oil companies):** In 1948, C.M. Nair, a Malayali employee of BAPCO (Bahrain Petroleum Co.), filed a complaint that "employees from European countries were paid twice as much as an Indian doing the same job." — [Ilias, OMER Journal 2018](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- Malabar Muslim dock workers (khalasis) brought their maritime skills to Gulf ports, especially Sharjah. Their work was said to "outshine other nationalities in their profession, especially Pakistanis." — [Ilias, OMER Journal 2018](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- Onmanorama has a 2026 feature on the "Great Bombay Dream" of Malayali migration to Bombay. It was not fetched. — [Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/lifestyle/keralaspora/2026/03/22/malayalis-community-bombay-history.html)
- Scroll has a separate article on the forgotten history of Malayali workers in Sri Lanka. It was not fetched. — [Scroll](https://scroll.in/article/1092587/the-forgotten-history-of-malayali-workers-in-sri-lanka-holds-lessons-for-migration-today)

### Inferences
- Script framing: the Gulf did not come out of nowhere. The oil boom ran along sea routes and family ties that were centuries old, which helps explain why Malappuram, Calicut and the Mappila community went first.
- Bombay was the springboard. Many early migrants worked there before getting on a launch to the Gulf (for example, Shoukkath worked in a Bombay provision store first, per the OMER paper).

### Gaps
- No sourced numbers for Malayalis in Ceylon or Malaya before 1950, and no sourced account of Ceylon's post-independence expulsions or the Malaya/Singapore return flows. Ceylon's citizenship laws (1948–49) and the 1964 Sirima–Shastri pact did push Indians out, but I did not verify how many Malayalis were affected.
- The Mappila community's own economic history (for example, marginalisation after the 1921 Malabar rebellion) was not covered by a source I fetched.

## 2. Triggers: the oil shock plus Kerala's own push factors

### Takeaway
The pull came from the 1973 oil price boom and the Gulf construction boom that followed. The push came from Kerala itself: land reforms that broke up estates, educated youth with few jobs, and a weak industrial base. Researchers call the 1973 oil boom the "watershed" of Kerala's largest migration stream. The link between militant trade unionism (nokkukooli) and low industrialisation is widely claimed but contested.

### Cited Findings
- Researchers describe Gulf emigration after the 1973 oil price boom as "a watershed" and Kerala's largest international migration stream. — [Revisiting Kerala's Gulf Connection 1972–2020, Indian Journal of Labour Economics (2020)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41027-020-00280-z)
- The "Gulf Boom" of mass Malayali migration is conventionally dated 1972–1983. — [Wikipedia: Kerala Gulf diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_Gulf_diaspora) (tertiary source)
- Land reform: the 1957 Communist government brought in land reform and "land to the tiller." Large landholdings broke up, upper-caste (savarna) families lost their base, and "money had begun to erode hierarchy." After losing agrarian wealth, educated upper-caste Malayalis looked for work all over India. This is the origin of the "Malayali on the moon" joke. — [Scroll](https://scroll.in/article/1068343/the-malayali-on-the-moon-and-fault-lines-of-caste-what-drove-generations-of-migration-from-kerala)
- **Anecdote:** Kunjabdulla, who migrated in the late 1960s, left because his family lost land through Kerala's land reforms. Balan, from the Ezhava community, cited caste-based job discrimination at home. — [Ilias, OMER Journal 2018](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- Unemployment: in the KMS sample, 58.8% of emigrants were employed before migrating and 85.8% after. Migration worked as the state's safety valve for unemployment. — [CDS Working Paper 483 (KMS 2018)](https://cds.edu/wp-content/uploads/WP483.pdf)
- Even after 50 years of remittances, "educated unemployment" is still unresolved, especially among women. — [IJLE 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41027-020-00280-z)
- Nokkukooli means "wages for (just) looking on." Unions extract payment for work they do not do. It is "cited as one of the reasons for poor industrial development in Kerala." CM Pinarayi Vijayan declared it abolished on May 1, 2018, but it persists; in 2021 unions blocked an ISRO equipment truck in Trivandrum. — [Wikipedia: Nokku kooli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nokku_kooli)
- **CONTESTED:** Kerala ministers have publicly called Union Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman's nokkukooli remarks "misleading" (headline only, not fetched) — [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/india/kerala/kerala-minister-slams-nirmala-sitharamans-nokkukooli-remarks-as-misleading-3451978). The Kerala High Court has observed that investors are wary of nokkukooli (headline only) — [Business Standard via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/india/business-standard/20210911/281689732933259)
- The migration-prone age group (15–29) fell from 52.7% of the labour force in 1981 to 36.9% in 2011. Kerala reached replacement-level fertility in 1987. This matters for later phases, because the pool of young people who might emigrate shrank. — [CDS WP 483](https://cds.edu/wp-content/uploads/WP483.pdf)

### Inferences
- The strongest script line is "a literate Kerala with no factories met an illiterate-labour-hungry Gulf with money." This is a common framing, not a cited quote.
- The trade-union/nokkukooli explanation should be presented as one contested strand, not the cause. The land-reform, caste and demographic explanations have firmer academic backing in the sources above.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch a source for the exact provisions of the Kerala Land Reforms (Amendment) Act 1969, which came into force on 1 January 1970. The 1957 reform framing above is from Scroll.
- No sourced numbers for educated unemployment rates in 1970s Kerala, for population density, or for agricultural stagnation (for example, falling paddy area). CDS papers from the 1970s–80s (such as Prakash's) would have these.

## 3. Early channels: dhows, "launches", NOCs, agents, Khor Fakkan

### Takeaway
From the 1940s to the late 1960s, much of the migration was undocumented. Migrants went on dhows, cargo boats and smuggling "launches" (the "kalla kappal") from Calicut, Ponnani and Bombay to Khor Fakkan, Sharjah and Sohar. Some were dropped offshore and swam to land. Some died on the way. Formal visas, NOCs and recruitment agents took over in the 1970s.

### Cited Findings
- First wave, 1940s–50s: undocumented migrants left Calicut and Ponnani via Bombay, Karachi and Gwadar. Second wave, 1960s: migrants travelled on dhows and country craft (uru) carrying cargo such as onions, dates, coir and fish oil. By the late 1960s and early 1970s, immigration rules were formalised and undocumented crossings became harder. — [Ilias, OMER Journal 2018](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- Launches from Bombay took 9 to 22 days to reach Khor Fakkan or Sohar. In one account of a 22-day trip, "fourteen passengers could not complete their journey as they died on the way." — [Ilias](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- Bombay agents charged about Rs 400 (about USD 6). — [Ilias](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- **1965 launch disaster:** the Dubai Coast Guard chased an undocumented launch from Bombay. The tow rope broke, the boat capsized, and "more than thirty people" died. Witnesses recalled different dates. — [Ilias](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- Early pay: a cook earned about Rs 100 (about USD 1.50). The paper says this was "then higher than senior government salaries in Kerala". That comparison looks odd, so check the original before using it. Housing was tents or "chopadas" (tin and plastic shacks) with 10–15 people per room. — [Ilias](https://omerjournal.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/vol-2-no-1-trinity-2018-ilias.pdf)
- "Kalla kappal" ("thief ship") journeys lasted days or months, without documents. Passengers were dropped offshore and swam the rest of the way. — [The News Minute](https://www.thenewsminute.com/kerala/how-boat-journey-mid-1960s-started-kerala-s-gulf-boom-130847)
- **Anecdote, Shahjahan:** he pawned or sold his sister's jewellery to buy a Rs 5,000 NOC and reached Dubai in 1975. He earned 1,000 dirhams a month and sent home 500 (over Rs 10,000 in today's money, per the article). He was jailed for two years in the 1978 crackdown for visa violations. His brother Kidher Mohammed bailed him out and went to Abu Dhabi himself in 1978. Their father Hyder was a fisherman. — [The News Minute (2020)](https://www.thenewsminute.com/kerala/how-boat-journey-mid-1960s-started-kerala-s-gulf-boom-130847)
- **Anecdote, Yusuff Ali (Lulu):** he arrived on 31 December 1973 at age 18, after a six-day voyage from Bombay on the ship *Dumra*, and landed at Rashid Port, Dubai. Getting from Dubai to Abu Dhabi took about four hours by pickup truck on a single-track road. He started in his uncle's distribution business. — [Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/from-working-at-small-shop-to-billionaire-boss-lulus-yusuffali-completes-50-year-dream-run-in-uae)
- The Varnam Substack essay "The Dhow to Khor Fakkan" retells this route. It was not fetched. — [Varnam](https://varnam.substack.com/p/the-dhow-to-khor-fakkan)

### Inferences
- The film *Pathemari* (2015, Mammootty) dramatises exactly this dhow-to-Khor-Fakkan crossing. The film is listed in [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_Gulf_diaspora), but I did not fetch a plot source.
- The dhow years (1950s–60s) were mostly Muslim, from Malabar. The 1970s visa era broadened migration to Hindus and Christians from southern Kerala as well.

### Gaps
- Kafala: I did not fetch a sourced description of the sponsorship system or when it was codified.
- First scheduled flights between Kerala and the Gulf (for example, Trivandrum or Calicut to the Gulf, and Calicut airport's 1988 opening): not verified.
- "Gulf pocket" districts are covered in Section 5 (Malappuram, Kannur, Kollam, Tirur taluk).

## 4. Phases: 1970s–80s boom, 1990 Kuwait, 2000s Dubai, 2010s nationalisation, COVID, and the shift West

### Takeaway
Emigrant numbers rose from about 1.4m (1998) to a peak of about 2.4m (2013), fell to about 2.1m (2018), and rose again to about 2.2m (2023). The Gulf's share fell from 89.2% to 80.5%. Remittances kept rising regardless. The main shocks were the 1990 Kuwait invasion (the largest civilian airlift), Saudi Nitaqat (2011–13), and COVID, which sent 15 lakh people back to Kerala between May 2020 and July 2021.

### Cited Findings
**1972–1983 boom**
- The first boom peaked around 1980. Kerala's population then was about 2.3 crore. — [The News Minute](https://www.thenewsminute.com/kerala/how-boat-journey-mid-1960s-started-kerala-s-gulf-boom-130847)

**1990 Kuwait invasion**
- More than 170,000 Indians were evacuated between 13 August and 20 October 1990. The actual flights ran from 18 August to 20 October, 63 days. Air India and Indian Airlines flew 488 flights. Evacuees went by bus via Basra and Baghdad to Amman, then flew 4,117 km to Bombay. Foreign Minister I.K. Gujral secured Iraqi cooperation. Kuwait-based Indians Mathunny Mathews (a Malayali, nicknamed "Toyota Sunny"), Harbajan Singh Vedi, Abey Varicad, N.V.K. Warrier and others organised things on the ground. Air India entered the Guinness Book for the most people evacuated by a civil airliner. — [Wikipedia: 1990 airlift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_airlift_of_Indians_from_Kuwait); [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/article/news-ians/how-air-india-s-1990-airlift-created-a-guinness-record-116020201070_1.html); [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/india/three-decades-before-covid-india-conducted-the-largest-civilian-evacuation-in-history/416095/)
- The 2016 Hindi film *Airlift* (Akshay Kumar) is loosely based on these events. — [Wikipedia: Airlift (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airlift_(film))

**2000s boom**
- Emigrants rose from 1.32m (1998) to 2.19m (2008), a 61% rise. Remittances rose from Rs 13,652 crore (1998) to Rs 43,288 crore (2008). — [CDS WP 483](https://cds.edu/wp-content/uploads/WP483.pdf)
- In 2008, remittances were 31% of NSDP (Net State Domestic Product). — [MEI](https://mei.edu/publication/remittances-kerala-impact-economy/)
- The 2008 financial crisis slowed later migration. — [Wikipedia: Kerala Gulf diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_Gulf_diaspora)

**2010s: nationalisation and the peak**
- Saudi Arabia's Nitaqat (2011) triggered return migration. Similar policies followed elsewhere: Bahrainisation, Qatarisation, Emiratisation. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_Gulf_diaspora)
- During the 2013 Saudi amnesty, more than 134,000 Indian workers returned. Only 13,714 of them were Keralites as of the report date, far fewer than the "mass exodus" feared. A state survey put Keralites in Saudi Arabia at 450,229. — [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/gulf/saudi/134000-indians-return-from-saudi-arabia-1.1251754). The page shows a 2018 date stamp, but the content is about the 2013 amnesty.
- Emigration peaked at 2.40m in 2013 and fell to 2.12m in 2018 (down 278,488). The causes cited: fewer young people, falling Gulf wages, rising Kerala wages, the post-2010 oil price slump, Nitaqat and family taxes. The authors frame this as a "migration hump" (an inverted U curve). — [CDS WP 483](https://cds.edu/wp-content/uploads/WP483.pdf)
- Remittances peaked at about 23.5% of NSDP around 2013–14 and fell to 13.3% by 2020. — [IJLE 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41027-020-00280-z)

**COVID (2020–21)**
- From May 2020 to 3 July 2021, 15,01,326 NRIs returned to Kerala. By country: UAE 8,90,485; Saudi 1,73,561; Qatar 1,47,917; Oman 1,36,445; Kuwait 52,032; Bahrain 44,246; non-Gulf 56,640. Of these, 10,73,673 cited job loss as the reason. — [Onmanorama, citing NORKA](https://www.onmanorama.com/news/kerala/2021/07/06/nri-return-expats-covid-gulf-norka-data.html)
- Over 3 lakh people had registered with the state to return by early May 2020. — [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/asia/india/covid-19-over-300000-of-kerala-diaspora-register-to-return-1.1588181023090)
- In KMS 2023, the top reasons for return were COVID job loss (18.4%), wanting to work in Kerala (16.1%) and retirement (12.1%). Return migrants rose from 1.2m (2018) to 1.8m (2023). — [StudyIQ summary of KMS 2023](https://www.studyiq.com/articles/kerala-migration-survey/); [KMS 2023 report](https://iimad.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/KMS-2023-Report.pdf)

**Post-2020 shift to the West**
- The GCC's share of emigrants fell from 89.2% (2018) to 80.5% (2023). The UAE is still the top destination at 38.6%, followed by Saudi Arabia at 16.9%. — [KMS 2023](https://iimad.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/KMS-2023-Report.pdf)
- Student emigrants doubled from 129,763 (2018) to about 2.5 lakh (2023), 11.3% of all emigrants. Women are 45.6% of student emigrants. — [StudyIQ/KMS 2023](https://www.studyiq.com/articles/kerala-migration-survey/)
- The UK's share of emigrants rose from 6.8% (2020–21) to 10.8% (2023–24). Kerala's share of India's GCC emigration clearances fell from 8.16% (2021) to 4.40% (2024). — [The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/economy/gulf-dreams-to-global-pathways-how-keralas-migration-economy-transitioned-in-the-last-decade)
- Women rose from 15.8% of emigrants (2018) to 19.1% (2023). 71.5% of women emigrants are graduates, against 34.7% of men, and 40.5% of women emigrants live in Western countries. — [The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/economy/gulf-dreams-to-global-pathways-how-keralas-migration-economy-transitioned-in-the-last-decade)

### Inferences
- The story arc is "from dhows to degrees." The migrant profile has moved from unskilled Malabar men on launches to graduate women (nurses and students) heading to the UK, Canada and Australia.
- The Gulf is not "over". It still takes about 80% of emigrants. But the growth is elsewhere.

### Gaps
- Nurse migration in particular: no sourced numbers for nurses to the UK, Ireland or elsewhere. The women's figures above are a proxy.
- The 2020 Vande Bharat numbers specific to Kerala were not verified. NORKA's total return count is used instead.
- The 2003 KMS figure (about 1.84m) was not sourced in these notes.

## 5. Transformation: economy, society, demography, religion and community, politics, culture

### Takeaway
Remittances turned Kerala from a low-income state into one of India's high per-capita-income states. They were 31% of NSDP in 2008, and KMS puts them at 23.2% of NSDP in 2023 (Rs 2.17 lakh crore). They paid for concrete "Gulf houses", consumer goods, education and health, and they especially lifted Malabar Muslims. The costs included inequality (only 12–17% of households receive remittances), about 1 million "Gulf wives", an ageing population, and dependence on the Gulf.

### Cited Findings
**Economy**
- The KMS remittance series: Rs 13,652 crore (1998), Rs 43,288 crore (2008), Rs 71,142 crore (2013), Rs 85,092 crore (2018), Rs 2,16,893 crore (2023, up 154.9%). — [CDS WP 483](https://cds.edu/wp-content/uploads/WP483.pdf); [KMS 2023](https://iimad.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/KMS-2023-Report.pdf)
- In 2023, remittances were 23.2% of NSDP (13.5% in 2018). Per-capita remittance was Rs 61,118, and the average per emigrant household was Rs 2.24 lakh (Rs 96,185 in 2018). — [The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/economy/gulf-dreams-to-global-pathways-how-keralas-migration-economy-transitioned-in-the-last-decade)
- **CONTESTED:** the IJLE paper puts remittances at 13.3% of NSDP in 2020. The jump to 23.2% in 2023 is partly a methods and rupee-depreciation effect, and it differs from RBI-based estimates. Present it as the KMS estimate. — [IJLE 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41027-020-00280-z)
- RBI's data give Kerala 19.7% of India's remittances in 2023–24 ($23.39bn), second to Maharashtra at 20.5%. — [FLAME / media report](https://www.flame.edu.in/in-the-media/nri-deposits-in-kerala-banks-head-towards-rs-3-trillion-mark)
- In 2008, remittances were 1.74 times the state's revenue receipts, 5.5 times what Kerala got from the Centre, and 2.3 times non-plan expenditure. They were enough to wipe out 70% of the state's debt, and equal to 36 times cashew export earnings and 30 times marine exports. Per-capita income was Rs 41,814 without remittances and Rs 54,664 with them. — [Middle East Institute (Zachariah & Irudaya Rajan)](https://mei.edu/publication/remittances-kerala-impact-economy/)
- NRI bank deposits crossed Rs 1 lakh crore in December 2014, Rs 2 lakh crore in March 2020, and stood at Rs 2,86,063 crore on 31 December 2024. — [FLAME / media report](https://www.flame.edu.in/in-the-media/nri-deposits-in-kerala-banks-head-towards-rs-3-trillion-mark); [Business Standard 2015](https://www.business-standard.com/article/news-ians/nri-deposits-in-kerala-banks-cross-rs-1-lakh-crore-115062200420_1.html)
- The limits of the model: remittances go to "a small segment of total households". Income and consumption inequality rose, and the tax-to-income ratio did not improve. — [IJLE 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41027-020-00280-z)
- Only 17.1% of households received cash remittances in 2008, and 12% did in 2023 (16% in 2018). — [MEI](https://mei.edu/publication/remittances-kerala-impact-economy/); [The Wire](https://m.thewire.in/article/economy/gulf-dreams-to-global-pathways-how-keralas-migration-economy-transitioned-in-the-last-decade)
- How remittances were used in 2023: house or shop renovation 15.8%, repaying bank loans 14%, education 10%, medical 7.7%, daily expenses 6.9%. — [StudyIQ/KMS 2023](https://www.studyiq.com/articles/kerala-migration-survey/)

**Housing and consumer culture**
- "Thatched, single-roomed huts were replaced by concrete homes." Migrants brought back perfumes, tape recorders, cameras and duty-free goods. Foreign cigarettes (555, Rothmans, Dunhill) replaced beedis. The author's father built the village's first concrete house in the late 1970s after five years in Abu Dhabi. — [Scroll](https://scroll.in/article/1064100/exaggerations-silences-and-treasure-keralas-arabian-tale-of-migration-to-the-gulf)
- Kathaprasangam (sung storytelling) performances exaggerated Gulf life. A departure ritual: migrants did not look back when they left. — [Scroll](https://scroll.in/article/1064100/exaggerations-silences-and-treasure-keralas-arabian-tale-of-migration-to-the-gulf)

**Religion and community (Malabar Muslims)**
- In 2023, Muslims were 41.9% of emigrants against 29.3% of the population (per the KMS report; other sources say about 26%). Hindus were 35.2% and Christians 22.3%. — [KMS 2023](https://iimad.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/KMS-2023-Report.pdf). *Warning:* StudyIQ's summary misprints the Hindu share as "2%". Use 35.2%.
- In 2008, Muslim households received Rs 19,000 crore (average Rs 1,19,004), Hindu households Rs 16,493 crore (average Rs 37,385), and Christian households Rs 7,800 crore (average Rs 50,107). — [MEI](https://mei.edu/publication/remittances-kerala-impact-economy/)
- Malappuram had 377,647 emigrants in 2023, the most of any district. Tirur taluk alone has about 100,000. In 2018, Malappuram had 42.1 emigrants per 100 households, followed by Kannur (38.8) and Kollam (32.8), and received 20.6% of the state's remittances. — [KMS 2023](https://iimad.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/KMS-2023-Report.pdf); [CDS WP 483](https://cds.edu/wp-content/uploads/WP483.pdf)

**Demography and gender: "Gulf wives"**
- A 2008 CDS survey counted about 1.06 million "Gulf wives" in Kerala. In the study by Dr S. Irudaya Rajan, loneliness was the main problem, followed by handling medical emergencies alone. 60% wished their husbands were home, and 40% felt the money outweighed the separation. 83% preferred future husbands to work in the Gulf. Researchers also found that women who ran the household "became more confident and independent." Human anecdote from the same piece: Rasiya Moideen Koya's husband worked 32 years in Abu Dhabi. — [The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/arts/the-gulf-wives-of-kerala-1.550611)
- Caregiving for elderly parents in emigrant households falls mostly on women. — [Asian Population Studies (Taylor & Francis)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17441730.2017.1412593) (abstract-level; not fetched)

**Politics**

### Inferences
- The "Kerala model puzzle": high HDI with low production growth in the 1970s–80s, and then fast growth from the late 1980s. Remittances plausibly explain the later catch-up, because consumption and construction rose rather than manufacturing (per the IJLE framing). Present this as the scholarly argument, not a settled fact.
- Muslim households got roughly three times the average remittance of Hindu households in 2008. This is a strong data point for the "Malabar Muslim uplift" story.
- The recurring "Gulf returnee who loses everything" story (in films and in the Loka Kerala Sabha's attention to returnees) reflects the weak local investment channel.
