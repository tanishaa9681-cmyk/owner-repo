# The Gulf War Is Choking Kerala, and the Bank Data Is Hiding It

*Runtime: about 12–13 min at a steady pace. Lines in [brackets] are on-screen or B-roll cues and aren't spoken.*

---

## COLD OPEN

28 February 2026. The US and Israel strike Iran. Iranian missiles hit the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Kuwait. The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel that carries much of the world's oil, gets choked.

Three thousand kilometres away, what happens to bank deposits in Kerala?

They go **up**. By 31 March, NRI deposits in Kerala's banks hit a record ₹3.24 lakh crore.

If that sounds like good news, sit down. When the people inside a burning building start sending their savings home, that isn't prosperity. It's an evacuation. The money leaves first, and the people follow.

Today: how a Gulf war reaches a household in Malappuram, why the healthy numbers are the scariest part, and why young Keralites will keep leaving anyway.

---

## 1. HOW BIG IS THE PIPE?

Start with size. The Kerala Migration Survey 2023 puts the money sent home to Kerala at ₹2.17 lakh crore a year. That's about **₹594 crore every single day**.

That's 1.7 times the state government's revenue receipts. The diaspora out-earns the treasury.

Kerala has about 3% of India's population and receives about a fifth of India's remittances: 19.7% in the RBI's 2023-24 survey, second only to Maharashtra.

The number that actually matters, though, is concentration. In 2016-17, 46.7% of India's remittances came from the Gulf. By 2023-24 that was down to 37.9%, and the US alone was sending 27.7%. **India diversified.**

Kerala didn't. **80.5%** of its emigrants still work in the six Gulf countries, and the UAE alone has 38.6% of them.

Any portfolio review would flag this: one region, 80% concentration, and that region is now a war zone.

A caveat, because I'd rather be precise than dramatic: remittances are somewhere between 13% and 23% of state income, depending on how you measure. Either way, this is a remittance economy.

## 2. HOW DID KERALA END UP HERE?

Kerala's trade with Arabia goes back to the first century. The real turning point, though, was **1973**. The oil shock quadrupled crude prices, and suddenly the Gulf had more money than hands to build its airports, roads and cities.

At home, Kerala had every reason for people to leave. Land reforms broke up the big estates, and many families lost their farm incomes. Literacy was high, so ambitions were high, but the jobs weren't there. For lower castes, the Gulf offered hard work without the contempt they faced at home.

So people went, and often not by legal or comfortable routes. Launches from Bombay to Khor Fakkan could take 22 days. On one trip, fourteen passengers died. The *kalla kappal*, the "thief ship", dropped people offshore and they swam to the coast.

Two stories show the range of outcomes.

In 1975, **Shahjahan** sold his sister's jewellery to pay for a ₹5,000 travel permit. He earned 1,000 dirhams a month, sent 500 home, and was jailed in the 1978 visa crackdown.

On 31 December 1973, an 18-year-old named **Yusuff Ali** landed in Dubai after six days on a ship called the *Dumra*. Today his Lulu Group runs more than 260 hypermarkets and malls across 24 countries.

Same route, very different endings. Remember that spread between them, because it's the whole Kerala story.

---

## 3. WHAT GULF MONEY BUILT, AND WHAT IT DIDN'T

Remittances grew roughly sixteenfold between 1998 and 2023. That money turned thatched huts into concrete "Gulf houses" and paid for schools and hospitals. In 2008, Malabar's Muslim households, long left behind, received about **three times** the average remittance of Hindu households. That's social mobility, and no government scheme paid for it.

Today, rural Kerala spends ₹6,611 per person per month, the highest among the major states. The rural Indian average is ₹4,122.

But the top uses of remittances in 2023 were renovation, loan repayment and education, with business nowhere near the top. Kerala built a consumption engine that runs on imported fuel. The 2011 Census counted **11.9 lakh houses standing empty**. Gulf money built the houses, but it didn't build the jobs that would let anyone live in them.

A 2008 survey counted about 10.6 lakh "Gulf wives" running homes alone, and 83% still wanted their future husbands in the Gulf. That's economics, not romance.

---

## 4. THE WAR, IN THREE LINES

In 2015-16 the Gulf slowed because oil was *cheap*. This time oil is *expensive*, and the missiles are hitting hotels, airports and ports instead of government balance sheets. **The governments are rich and the service economies are frightened**, and Keralites work in the hotel, not the finance ministry.

The rising deposits from the opening are workers sending their savings home on the way out. Inflows into NRE accounts, where ordinary workers save, fell about **91%** in April–July. Migration economist S. Irudaya Rajan *projects* a 20% drop in remittances, about ₹43,000 crore a year, or roughly **₹44,800 for every household that receives Gulf money**.

The more interesting question is who ends up paying for that.

---

## 5. FOLLOW THE MONEY: WHO ACTUALLY PAYS?

When a Dubai hotel lays off a Malayali waiter, who in India loses? Follow the rupee down the chain.

**Link one: a few districts, not the whole state.** Only about **12%** of Kerala households receive cash remittances. Malappuram alone sends 17.5% of Kerala's emigrants, and Kollam takes nearly 18% of the money. So the statewide average hides a recession concentrated in a handful of districts. Kerala's GSDP will barely flinch while Tirur feels it in every shop.

**Link two: the building site.** The top uses of remittances in 2023 were house and shop renovation (15.8%) and loan repayment (14%). Gulf money is effectively Kerala's private construction budget. When a family's income is at risk, the new floor on the house is the first thing it cancels, and the loan instalment is the thing it can't cancel.

**Link three: the worker who isn't Malayali.** Who builds that floor? About **25 lakh workers from other states**, drawn in by India's highest construction wage of about ₹838 a day. When renovation stops, the mason from Murshidabad loses work before anyone in Kerala does. **Kerala exports the shock.** A war in the Gulf turns up in a village in Bengal, Assam or Odisha, and it shows up in no Kerala statistic at all.

**Link four: the banks.** Kerala's banks hold about ₹3.24 lakh crore of NRI deposits. The worker's paycheck is the steady, low-cost part of that money, while the big foreign-currency deposits chase whatever rate is on offer and leave when it's gone. Banks that built their funding on the Gulf worker will see it in their deposit mix long before it shows up in any loan book.

**Link five, and last: the state.** The direct tax loss is only about ₹3,000 crore. The state is hit last and least.

So the shock runs downhill. The Gulf government barely notices, Kerala's state budget takes a small hit, and the remittance household loses a school fee. The migrant labourer at the end of the chain loses his whole income. The poorest person in the chain pays first, and he isn't even from Kerala.

## 6. WE'VE BEEN HERE BEFORE

**1990.** Iraq invades Kuwait, and India airlifts more than 1.5 lakh people, a Guinness record. The hit to India's foreign-exchange reserves fed straight into the 1991 crisis, the one where India pledged its gold. Kerala helped fewer than 4,000 of the returnees.

**2013.** Saudi Arabia's Nitaqat rules set quotas for hiring locals, and everyone predicted a mass exodus. About 13,700 Keralites came home, out of roughly 4.5 lakh in the country. That's about 3%.

**2014–16.** The oil crash quietly did the real damage: Kerala's emigrant count fell from 24 lakh to 21 lakh, with no evacuation at all.

Sudden shocks make headlines, but slow shocks shrink the base, and this war could do both. The response has followed the same pattern every time: the Centre evacuates, the state pays relief, and everyone waits for the next flight out.

---

## 7. THE SAFETY-NET AUDIT

To be fair, Kerala has India's most developed system for returning emigrants: NORKA Roots, welfare-board pensions and distress relief.

The problem is scale. Kerala has **18 lakh** returned emigrants. The flagship business scheme for returnees, NDPREM, funded about 10,500 ventures between 2016 and 2025. That's roughly **0.6%** of returnees. In FY24 it reached about 1,200 people, and a Kochi think tank found that many returnees didn't know it existed.

The distress-relief scheme, Santhwana, paid out roughly twice as much. The system is built to console people, not to help them start over.

The June 2026 budget announced a Global Pravasi Rehabilitation Scheme, a Pravasi Investment Company and a Global Placement Cell, with no money allocated to any of them. The names are great, though.

---

## 8. WHY THEY'LL STILL GO

This paradox should bother every economist. **Kerala pays India's highest manual wages.** Rural construction pays ₹838 a day, against ₹373 nationally. The pay is good enough that about 25 lakh workers from other states come to Kerala to do these jobs.

Yet youth unemployment in Kerala is **29%**, against about 10% nationally. For young women it's **47%**, and graduate unemployment is among the highest in India.

Kerala has two labour markets. The manual one pays well but carries no status for a graduate, and the white-collar one simply has too few seats. So the graduate leaves, and a worker from Bengal or Assam takes the job the graduate was never going to do.

The Gulf isn't the dream anymore. It's the backup plan.

The mix is shifting too. Destinations outside the Gulf nearly doubled to 19.5% of emigrants in five years, and students abroad doubled to about 2.5 lakh. Keralites aren't choosing to stay. They're just leaving from a different airport.

---

## 9. THE CASE AGAINST MY OWN ARGUMENT

Now let me argue against myself.

**One:** high oil means rich Gulf governments. If security returns, rebuilding could set off a hiring boom.

**Two:** past fears of an exodus were overblown, and most workers stay unless they're fired.

**Three:** the weak rupee, near ₹96.6 to the dollar in May, means every dirham sent home buys more, which cushions the fall.

All fair points. None of them explains a 91% collapse in the account where workers keep their savings. That isn't a forecast. It has already happened.

---

## CLOSE

So is the Gulf war choking Kerala? It isn't showing up in the treasury or the headlines yet. What it's choking is the source, meaning the jobs that produce the money. The headline numbers will look healthy for a few more quarters, precisely because people are emptying their savings on the way out.

If you want to track this, ignore total deposits. Watch NRE inflows, and watch Kerala's share in the next RBI remittance survey. That's where the truth will show up first.

Fifty years ago, Kerala solved unemployment by exporting it, and every Gulf crisis since has been the bill for that. Kerala keeps paying it the same way, with the next flight out.
