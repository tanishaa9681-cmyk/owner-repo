# Kerala Exported Its Unemployment. Now the Gulf Is Sending It Back.

*Final script. Lines in [brackets] are cues and aren't spoken. Figures to check before recording are listed at the end.*

---

## OPENING: THE THESIS

On 28 February this year, missiles started falling on the Gulf: the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait.

Three thousand kilometres away, Kerala began doing the maths. S. Irudaya Rajan, the migration economist who has tracked Kerala's emigrants for three decades, projects that the money they send home will fall by about 20%. That's roughly ₹43,000 crore in a single year.

The usual explanation is that Kerala depends too much on the Gulf. That's true, but it's the shallow version.

Here's my argument. Gulf money didn't just make Kerala dependent. **It is the reason Kerala has nothing else to depend on.** The same money that pulled Kerala out of poverty made it too expensive to build an economy at home. So every time the Gulf shakes, Kerala has no other job market to fall back on.

To see why, we have to go back to where it started.

---

## PART 1: 1973, THE DEAL

In 1973, oil prices quadrupled. Almost overnight, the Gulf had enormous money and very few people, and it needed airports, roads and whole cities built.

Kerala had the opposite problem. It had India's highest literacy, schools that worked, and a generation of healthy, educated young men, but almost no factories to employ them.

One side had jobs without workers, the other had workers without jobs, and the match was made.

Note how families paid for it. The 1998 migration survey found that going abroad cost about ₹44,000, and the visa was the biggest cost. Most families put that money together from several sources: sixty per cent took a loan from a bank or moneylender, half borrowed from relatives, forty per cent sold gold, and very few sold land.

[ON SCREEN: "Families used more than one source, so these add up to more than 100%"]

So a Gulf job was never a windfall. It was a loan taken against a salary that hadn't been earned yet, and the family came out ahead only as long as the job lasted.

Keep that in mind. It's the first weakness, and it's been there since day one.

---

## PART 2: THE 1980s AND 90s, WHAT THE MONEY DID

The money arrived, and it did two things at once.

**The first is the part everyone celebrates.** Thatched huts became concrete houses. Malabar's Muslim families, long the poorest in the state, rose fastest; by 2008 they were receiving about three times the average remittance of Hindu households. Today Kerala has India's lowest poverty rate, 0.55% on NITI Aayog's 2023 index, and its rural households spend more per person than those of any other major state.

**The second is the part nobody celebrates.** Remittances arrive as household income, not as investment, and households spend them.

You might say that's fine. Spending is demand, and somebody has to meet that demand. True, but look at who meets it.

Part of the money buys goods: gold, cars, phones, appliances. Very little of that is made in Kerala, so the money leaves the state almost as soon as it arrives, and the factories it pays for are in Tamil Nadu, Gujarat or abroad.

The rest buys things that have to be supplied locally: land, houses, weddings, schooling, hospital care. That does create jobs, but of two kinds. Shops, schools and hospitals sell only to Kerala, so they can only grow as fast as the remittances do. And construction work is manual labour that, as we'll see, Keralites increasingly won't take.

Meanwhile, all that local spending bids up the price of the two things every factory needs: land and labour.

So land prices rose. Wages rose too, because a family with a son in Dubai won't send another son out to work for ₹400 a day.

Economists call this the Dutch disease. When the Netherlands found gas in the 1960s, the money raised costs so much that Dutch manufacturing quietly died. Kerala caught the same disease, except that its export was people.

Today rural construction in Kerala pays about ₹838 a day, against ₹373 for India. Try running a factory where land is scarce and labour is the most expensive in the country. The money didn't just fail to build industry. **It priced industry out.**

So the problem was never a lack of demand. The demand was met by factories in other states, by local businesses that sell only to Kerala, and later by workers from outside. The costs, meanwhile, landed on anyone trying to make something in Kerala and sell it elsewhere.

That's the core of the argument: remittances solved Kerala's poverty problem and created its jobs problem, with the same money.

---

## PART 3: 1990, THE FIRST WARNING

The first test came early.

In August 1990, Iraq invaded Kuwait. India airlifted more than 1.5 lakh people in about two months, still one of the largest civilian evacuations in history.

Now look at it from Kerala's side. Economist B.A. Prakash counts about 1.8 lakh Indians repatriated from Kuwait and Iraq that year, and more than one lakh of them were Keralites. More than half of everyone India brought home was going to one state.

A 1992 study of the crisis by the economist Ashwani Saith found that the damage got worse at every level you looked at: "more acute as the focus shifts from the level of the macroeconomy, to the state of Kerala, to the migrants themselves." For India, 1990 was a foreign-exchange problem that fed into the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis. For Kerala, it was one lakh households losing their income at once.

And Kerala's own government managed to help fewer than 4,000 of those returnees, roughly one in twenty-five.

Then the war ended, the Gulf reopened, and people went back. Kerala's remittances, about ₹1,310 crore in 1990, reached ₹5,539 crore by 1995, more than four times as much in five years. The money came back so fast that the crisis was easy to forget.

In 2000, Kerala's migration survey put the lesson in writing. It said the state was "too much dependent on migration," because migration "could stop abruptly, as was shown by the Kuwait war experience of 1990."

So why did nothing change?

Because the arrangement suited everyone. Families got incomes, and the state collected tax on everything those families spent without having to create a single job. Remittances didn't just fund Kerala's consumption. **They let Kerala put off the hard work of building an economy.** Why fight over land, labour laws and investment when the next visa solves this year's unemployment?

In effect, the Gulf became Kerala's employment policy.

---

## PART 4: THE 2000s TO 2020, THE QUIET TESTS

Over the next two decades, the Gulf tested that policy several more times.

**2004.** The surveyors noticed something new. Kerala had itself become "a Gulf" for workers from other states. Keralites had left for Dubai, and workers from Bengal, Assam, Bihar and Odisha had arrived to take their place. Today there are about 25 lakh of them. The Dutch disease had come full circle: Kerala's wages were now too high for Keralites to accept manual work, but high enough to import workers from the rest of India.

**2013.** Saudi Arabia's Nitaqat rules forced companies to hire locals. About 13,700 Keralites came home, around 3% of those working there. It was survivable.

**2014 to 2016.** Oil crashed. There was no evacuation and no headlines, but Kerala's emigrant count quietly fell from 24 lakh to 21 lakh. This one mattered more than 1990, because slow shocks shrink the base while sudden ones only interrupt it.

**2020.** COVID sent 15 lakh Keralites home, and seven in ten had lost their jobs.

Every time, the pattern repeated. Delhi ran the evacuation, Kerala paid some relief, and then the returnees started looking for the next visa.

The state's own machinery shows it. Kerala today has 18 lakh returned emigrants. Its main scheme to help them start businesses reached about 1,200 people in 2023–24, with ₹18 crore. That's less than a tenth of one per cent of returnees in a year.

The system was never designed to bring people home. It was designed to wait until they could leave again.

---

## PART 5: 2023, WHERE KERALA STANDS

By the 2023 migration survey, the arrangement had reached its peak.

Kerala received ₹2.17 lakh crore in remittances that year, 1.7 times everything the state government collects in revenue. With about 3% of India's population, it received 19.7% of India's remittances. And 80.5% of its emigrants work in the six Gulf states.

Now compare Kerala with India. Over the same years, India moved away from the Gulf. The Gulf's share of India's remittances fell from 46.7% to 37.9%, and the United States alone now sends 27.7%.

Kerala moved the other way. Its share of India's remittances rose from 10.2% to 19.7% in three years.

**India diversified; Kerala doubled down, just before the Gulf caught fire.**

Why do Keralites still go? Go back to Part 2. Kerala now has two job markets. The manual one pays India's best wages, but graduates won't take those jobs. The white-collar one has too few seats. Youth unemployment in Kerala is 29%, against about 10% nationally, and for young women it's 47%.

So the graduate leaves, and a worker from Assam takes the job the graduate was never going to do. The Gulf isn't the dream anymore. It's the only exit the local economy offers.

Even the change underway is the same model. Students going abroad doubled to 2.5 lakh, and more women are leaving as nurses bound for Europe. The destinations are changing, but Kerala is still exporting people.

---

## PART 6: 2026, THE TEST

Which brings us to this year.

Remittances are normally the most reliable income in a crisis. When things go wrong at home, migrants send more. That works because the crisis and the job are in different countries.

This time the crisis is where the job is.

This shock also differs from 2015. That slowdown came from cheap oil, which emptied Gulf treasuries. This year Brent went from $80 to $126 and was still near $97 in September, so Gulf governments are flush.

What's being hit is physical: airports, hotels, ports, and shipping through Hormuz. By July, one in four UAE employers planned layoffs, concentrated in aviation, tourism, hospitality, construction and catering. That's precisely where Keralites work.

So the governments are richer than ever, and the hotels are empty.

Now trace a 20% cut through Kerala, using everything we've built up.

**The family.** Only 12% of Kerala households receive remittances, concentrated in Malappuram, Kollam and Kannur. So the cut doesn't spread thinly. It's about ₹44,800 a year for the average receiving household. Remember Part 1: many of those families are still repaying the loans that bought the visa.

**The worker from outside Kerala.** When a Gulf family cuts back, the renovation goes first. The man who loses that work is often a mason from Murshidabad. Kerala's shock lands in Bengal, and it never appears in Kerala's numbers.

**The state.** The government doesn't receive remittances; it only taxes what families spend. A 20% fall costs it roughly ₹3,000 crore. That's small on its own, but it lands on debt of ₹5.45 lakh crore, a third of GSDP, and a revenue deficit of ₹35,355 crore, which means the state already borrows to pay salaries and pensions.

And the response? In June, the new state budget announced a Global Pravasi Rehabilitation Scheme, a Pravasi Investment Company and a Global Placement Cell. It set aside no money for any of them.

In fairness, the other side has a case. If the fighting stops, high oil revenue could fund a Gulf rebuilding boom, and hiring could come back fast. Fears of an exodus were wrong in 2013 as well.

But notice what that argument is. It's the 1990 argument again: wait for the Gulf to reopen. If the war ends soon, Kerala will likely do exactly that, and it will have learned nothing for the second time.

---

## CONCLUSION: BACK TO THE THESIS

So, back to where we started.

Kerala's problem isn't that it depends on the Gulf. It's that fifty years of Gulf money made it too expensive to build anything else to depend on. Remittances ended poverty, raised land prices and wages, priced out industry, and turned emigration from a choice into the only exit. Each crisis since 1990 has tested that arrangement, and each time Kerala has answered with the next visa.

The bill keeps growing, too. In 2021, 16.5% of Keralites were over sixty, the highest share of any state, and that's projected to reach about 23% by 2036. Kerala educated its workers, the Gulf used their best years, and Kerala now gets them back in time for the pension and the hospital bill.

The Philippines runs a remittance economy on purpose. Remittances there are about 8% of GDP, and in 2021 it created a full cabinet ministry for migrant workers. Kerala is two to three times more exposed, and it has never decided whether migration is its strategy or its stopgap.

So that's the question for the comments. Should Kerala accept migration as its permanent industry and build proper institutions around it? Or should it use this crisis to do what it avoided after 1990, and build an economy that people don't have to leave?
